PBP 2027 Prep Series #3

A chorus of voices

Watch on YouTube ↗

Video: PBP 2027 Prep Series #3: "A chorus of voices" — Rob Hawks interviews Peter Curley, a RUSA (Randonneurs USA) member since October 2013, organizer of the St. Patrick's Day Massacre local brevet, two-time PBP finisher (2019, 2023), and creator of the YouTube channel "PBP Stories and Tips," where he has interviewed 50+ PBP riders and compiled their advice. (The episode's "chorus of voices" refers to this compilation of many interviewees' quotes and tips that Peter synthesizes, not multiple live guests on the call.)

Peter Curley's Background and Channel

  • Member of RUSA since October 2013; did his first PBP in 2019 with two friends, riding disorganized (no bike computer, insufficient clothing) but finished with dark moments.
  • Returned solo in 2023, aiming to fix prior issues; still encountered new issues — plans to return in 2027.
  • Runs "PBP Stories and Tips" YouTube channel: full-length interviews (50 minutes to an hour) with 50+ riders, each ending with the interviewee giving three tips that "guarantee a finish" at PBP; also runs a separate compilation "tips playlist" and a "what does PBP mean to you" video.
  • Also runs a second, unrelated YouTube channel, "Bike Every Hood," touring neighborhoods around San Francisco.
  • Notable interviewees mentioned: Deirdre Arscott (English, lives in Canada, has done 10 PBPs, one every four years since 1987); Jan Heine (Bicycle Quarterly), a fast rider with multiple Charlie Miller finishes across seven PBPs; Mark Thomas, who has ridden nearly 100,000 km of brevets worldwide, done seven PBPs, earned one Charlie Miller and one Ancien Randonneur award — Rob notes Mark may be missing an 84-hour-start PBP among his collection.

Six Universal Recommendations (from synthesizing 50+ interviews) 1. Understand your goals — decide first why you're riding (fast, slow, with friends, solo); what you put in determines what you get out. 2. Over-communicate with teammates if riding as a group — discuss control-stop plans 5 km out, and pre-agree on contingencies (e.g., what happens if someone can't continue). Peter's 2019 group wasted significant time at controls due to poor communication. 3. Every ride is a training ride — asks what you can learn from every ride (10 miles or 1,000 miles) between now and August 2027: sleep, nutrition, equipment. Cites "Lisa's" phrase: "train hard to ride easy." 4. Minimize time off the bike — described as the best bang-for-buck focus. Most "normal-ish" riders have a moving time of 50–60 hours; on a 90-hour start that leaves 30–40 hours off the bike. Track both total time and moving time on your bike computer, not just moving time. 5. Do not underestimate logistics stress — getting to the start line is often the hardest part. Anecdote: an Irish rider, highly organized with checklists, arrived at the start without his water bottles. Recommends: AirTag your luggage (Peter's saved him once when luggage was lost), arrive in France as early as possible, and do multiple shakeout rides pre-event to acclimate.

  • Rob's own anecdote: arriving late gave him no buffer for problems; arriving with two extra days on a later PBP made a big difference in feeling relaxed.

  • Peter's anecdote: in 2019 he locked himself out of his brother's apartment two days before the start with the only keyholder on vacation; had to fly his niece in from Ireland with a spare set of keys. 6. Expect things not to go according to plan — plan should have flexibility; pre-decide responses to likely failure scenarios since mid-ride decision-making degrades. Rule of thumb: if you want to quit, rest and eat first, then revisit the decision — never DNF on impulse. 7. Strive for "Type 1 fun" (added as a related/last point) — defined vs. Type 2 fun (miserable in the moment, great in retrospect) and Type 3 (regretted even afterward). Advice: stay in the moment, don't fixate on total distance remaining, focus instead on distance to the next control. Recommends interacting with locals/culture, and handing out pins to volunteers and kids at the roadside (Rob does this each cycle) as a way to create Type 1 fun and connect with locals despite language barriers.

  • Anecdote: Peter recalls giving a small boy (~8 years old) a Golden Gate Bridge–themed pin; Rob relates a story of a roadside conversation in broken French with a local near a café, aided by Jack Holmgren.

Rider-Type-Specific Advice

  • Fast / "Charlie Miller" types (those aiming for a sub-90-hour Charlie Miller award): apply the same six recommendations "but more so." Key focus: qualify for start waves A, B, or C by completing roughly 1,000 miles/1,000 km of brevets the year before.
    • Rob clarifies riders don't strictly "qualify" for a wave — pre-qualifiers let you pre-register and claim a wave, and A/B/C waves tend to fill fastest with speed-focused, pack-riding riders.
    • A Charlie Miller finish is achievable even from the 84-hour start — Rob knows someone who did it that way.
    • The "Charlie Miller Society" and "Ancien Randonneur" designations are described as largely American/RUSA-specific honors, though non-Americans are also inducted into the Ancien Randonneur ranks.
  • Slower / full-value "Ancien Randonneur" types: focus on savoring the event, interacting with locals and culture rather than speed.

Start Wave Structure (2027)

  • Three broad start-time groups: 80-hour, 90-hour, and 84-hour.
  • 80-hour and 90-hour groups start Sunday afternoon/evening; 90-hour is the largest group, 80-hour roughly twice the size of the 84-hour group.
  • 84-hour group starts before sunrise on Monday, in roughly 3–4 waves; it is the smallest group.
  • New for 2027: reported plan for a start wave specifically for women (timing relative to 80/90-hour groups not yet confirmed).
  • Rob's personal history: started 90-hour in 2007 (DNF'd, rained the entire time, only escaped rain briefly around Brest); switched to 84-hour in 2011, 2015, and 2019 (all finished under 80 hours actual time) and prefers it — attributes success to knowing he has a poor track record starting into the night, so an 84-hour morning start suits him better. Notes 84-hour starters gain roughly six more hours of sleep opportunity than a 90-hour starter.
  • Peter did 90-hour both times (2019, 2023) without deliberating; frames the 84-vs-90 tradeoff as giving up six hours of course time for six hours of sleep.
  • Sleep-strategy sub-discussion: no universal right answer.
    • Rob sleeps at Loudéac (same control each time — 2011, 2015, 2019) since it falls in the middle of the night phase; prefers not pushing further because of the stress of finding lodging.
    • Anecdote: Bruce Bird pushed past Loudéac outbound in 2007 rather than share a hotel room with Rob, rode further and slept elsewhere — worked for him but left Rob alone in a hotel room while DNFing.
    • Peter's first 600K: pre-booked hotels along the route didn't work out (nobody could sleep); his most enjoyable 600K happened when he convinced two riding companions to stop at a club's overnight control, though ironically he didn't end up sleeping there (stayed awake listening to them snore).
    • In 2023, Peter successfully took his first-ever "ditch nap," a breakthrough after previously being unable to sleep on the road at all.
    • Peter's 2023 start: left around 4 p.m., giving about five hours of daylight before riding through the night; started 90-hour again.

Closing Quotes Peter Shared

  • Eric Nichols (three-time PBP finisher): "Now that I've done three PBPs, I've come to realize that PBP is so much more than an athletic challenge — it is a very special cultural event. The preparation, the qualifying, all the sacrifices are just the cost of entry into a unique cultural experience. The ride is the backdrop; the people and the places are the centerpiece."
  • Greg (rode PBP 2023): "Every brevet is a lifetime compressed into a day" — meaning highs and lows constantly shift during the ride, but "dark times will pass, happy times will pass."
  • Rob adds that even repeating a similar route (e.g., PBP's near-identical 2011 and 2015 courses) feels completely different each time because the rider themselves has changed and brings more self-knowledge to later iterations.

Lightning-Round Q&A (Rob's five rapid-fire questions to Peter) 1. Going to PBP 2027? Yes. 2. Best bike for PBP? Peter's history: first PBP (2019) on a 25-year-old Cannondale-type frame with 23mm tires — great bike, but caused numbness; second (2023) on a steel Land Shark, also 23mm tires, same numbness issue. Plans to switch to thicker tires next time, expecting it to be a game-changer for comfort. Rob frames the takeaway as: don't chase a specific make/model, put in the work to find what fits you. 3. One thing you can't do without on PBP? The cultural experience — without the people, it's "just a ride through a place in France." 4. Best control? Peter can't recall the name but describes it as feeling like a "rock concert" — crowds, names called out over microphones, energy lining both sides of the street. In the past riders passed through it both outbound and inbound; for 2027, the route will only pass through it once, on the return. 5. Favorite food at the controls? Peter (vegetarian) says he's never struggled — simple staples like bread, cheese, and potatoes; specifically mentions potage (a thick soup) and purée (mashed potatoes) as reliable, if bland, options.
