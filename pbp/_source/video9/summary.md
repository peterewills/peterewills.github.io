**Video:** Randonneurs USA PBP Prep Interview Series #2 (2023) — Rob Hawks interviews Roger Hillas, a five-time PBP finisher (2003, 2007, 2011, 2015, 2019) and former president (four-year term) of the Washington, DC-area Randonneurs club (successor: Ed Felker; current RBA: Emily Ranson).

**Background & How He Got Into Randonneuring**
- Rode his first brevet in fall 2001, before joining RUSA; at the time was a member of the local club, Potomac Pedalers.
- In 2002 did an SR series and rode Boston-Montreal-Boston.
- Rode his first PBP in 2003 at age 50, finishing in 77 hours 25 minutes (84-hour start group). Training base was a daily 28-mile round-trip bike commute — no structured randonneuring base beforehand.
- Found PBP first via an article on the web, then worked backwards to figure out qualification — didn't realize a local DC randonneuring community already existed.
- Early club mentors: Dave Burning and Ken Zabalski, both of whom rode PBP in 1987; Dave still has Super-8 handlebar-mounted film from that ride. Ken did three more PBPs and mentored Hillas for his first.
- Prior cycling background: started riding bikes in the mid-1970s to get in shape for skiing; toured Nova Scotia in the 1980s, rode Central England to northern Scotland, and Southern England to Ivrea, Italy (crashed into a broken-down car there — broken nose and concussion, hospitalized).
- Started his career as a medievalist — cites his love of history and France as part of PBP's appeal.
- Has done all five of his PBPs from the first wave of the 84-hour group — values knowing where he needs to be at a given time as he's aged/slowed.

**PBP History and Weather by Year**
- 2003: Historic heat wave (100+ degrees in Paris) broke in a massive thunderstorm the night before the 90-hour start; the ride itself had glorious, sunny, not-too-hot weather throughout — describes it as one giant street festival with unusually large, cheerful crowds.
- 2007: Notoriously rainy year for the 80/90-hour starters; the 84-hour group rode on wet roads with only light rain — dampened spirits but not catastrophic.
- 2011: The 84-hour group got hit hard by violent afternoon thunderstorms near Villaines-la-Juhel on day one (four waves of storms, lots of lightning); fog the morning of day two leaving Loudéac, clearing on the return from Brest; sunny day three with a strong tailwind. Entered this PBP recovering from a fractured femoral neck (hit by another cyclist on his commute in November 2010) — hip surgery, 5.5 months off the bike, minimal training miles.
- 2019: Back pain set in badly on the final day — took the fun out of the ride's end, though he was close enough to the finish for it not to be a real problem.
- General: only one flat tire in five PBPs (2011). One notable mechanical failure in 2007 (below). Never had Shermer's neck or lasting injuries; occasional very sore saddle discomfort.

**Advice for First-Timers**
- Physical prep: nights in northwestern France can drop to the high 30s/low 40s°F even in August — pack more clothing than you think you need; it takes up space but doesn't add much weight.
- Mental/pacing: don't be rigidly wedded to a fixed plan — be flexible with weather. Push harder and bank time in good conditions/tailwinds; in headwinds, slow down rather than burn energy for little gain, since favorable conditions will return.
- Recommends practicing sleeping during a 600K brevet (rather than just riding through the night) to learn how to actually fall asleep while exhausted and wound up — sleep quality later helps a lot. Considers a night-start 400K useful for this too.
- Mechanical self-sufficiency matters: recounts fixing a kinked (not broken) 10-speed chain by headlamp between Carhaix and St-Méen-le-Grand in 2007 using a small chain tool, after his front derailleur geometry kept throwing the chain onto the big ring. No one stopped to help.
- Core mantra: "Stay calm — things are going to go bad, relax, getting excited doesn't help." Cites calming down a fellow rider melting down in a Loudéac food line in 2003 — "French lines don't move like American lines... it's part of the experience."
- Take time to stop and enjoy iconic spots rather than rushing: Paul Rocher's garage/crêpe stand after Fougères, the guy grilling galette-saucisse at the Loudéac control, fresh pâté at Tinténiac, the party atmosphere at Villaines-la-Juhel. Calls these "PBP moments" — always a few every ride.
- Recommends reading Graham Robb's "The Discovery of France" and Barry Cunliffe's "Bretons and Britons" (2001) to understand Brittany/Normandy's distinct regional identity — locals often identify as Norman or Breton rather than French. Anecdote: in 2003 asked a fellow rider "Vous êtes Français?" and got the calm reply "Je suis Breton."
- Route/regional trivia: castles between Villaines and Fougères; the France-Brittany border (a separate duchy until 1528) lies just east of Fougères; Fougères was a major 19th-century shoe-manufacturing center; Huelgoat (near Roc Trévezel, after Carhaix, through the national forest) was a silver-mining site 6,000 years ago and has old prehistoric fort remains, plus a small lake where Hillas has napped. Breton-language town names start appearing on signs alongside French ones. Key Breton vocabulary tip: "kouign-amann," the very caloric pastry, "perfect for randonneuring."

**Memorable Anecdotes**
- 2011, Châteauneuf-du-Faou (roughly halfway to Brest): declined a stranger's offer of coffee from a thermos to get food first at a patisserie; his friend John Gardner later got that same coffee from the same man, who had originally intended it for a friend he never found, then followed John along the course and picked him out of the crowd at the finish in Paris.
- 2011, third morning in Loudéac: friend Chip Adams' frame cracked; local bike shop mechanics couldn't fix it but lent him a bike to ride to the finish, arranging (via phone, with Klaus Klassen translating) to meet Chip in Paris and retrieve it; shop owners "Clarice and Pascal" met Chip at the finish, and Chip's photo reportedly still hangs on their wall.
- Made lasting friendships via randonneuring more broadly than specifically through PBP: David Leonard (Davis Bike Club, met 2002; their wives are close friends) and Mike Krast (met doing the Gold Rush Randonnée in 2017).
- Notes the 84-hour group begins overtaking 90-hour riders as early as Fougères (roughly 300K in) and increasingly throughout the rest of the route; by the time 84-hour riders reach Villaines-la-Juhel on the return, all the 90-hour riders seem to be there simultaneously.

**2023 Plans**
- Riding PBP 2023 in August — his sixth and, by his own statement, definitely his last, being four months short of his 70th birthday.
- Breaking from tradition: switching from the 84-hour start (which "gets down to business" fast, riding hard from the gun) to the 90-hour group, to experience that start style, acknowledging he'll lose time-banking advantages but wants the extra time cushion.
- Staying at the Mercure hotel right by the start line in Rambouillet, having booked via a reservation tip shared by Stacy Klein.
- Expects this PBP to be "a tour down memory lane," treasuring it especially given how good the weather was on his first, 2003.

**Closing Notes**
- Interview closes with Hillas and host Rob Hawks (himself a four-time PBP finisher: 2007, 2011, 2015, 2019) agreeing to look out for each other on course in 2023, given both are riding start groups that will cross paths (84-hour catching 90-hour riders en route).
