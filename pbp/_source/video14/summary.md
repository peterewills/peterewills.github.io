**Video:** Randonneurs USA Paris-Brest-Paris Prep Seminar #2 — hosted by Rob Hawks (RUSA), with presenters Max Paletta (80-hour start), Deb Banks (90-hour start), Mary Gersma (84-hour start), Mark Thomas (climbing/course profile), and a Q&A-style interview segment with Narayan Krishnamurthy (three-time PBP finisher).

**Host / Format**
- Rob Hawks has been a RUSA member since 2004, finished his first SR series in 2005, and rode PBP in 2007 (DNF), 2011, 2015, and 2019.
- Structure: each presenter covers a start-wave topic in PBP's actual starting order (80hr, then 90hr, then 84hr), audience submits questions via Q&A tagged with the presenter's name, and each presenter answers their top question at the end.
- Recording to be posted on YouTube; this is the second of the RUSA PBP prep seminar series.

**Max Paletta — 80-Hour Start**
- Randonneur since 2003 (New England Randonneurs), later ~10 years with San Francisco Randonneurs, created SFR's Adventure Series (200/300/400/600K mixed-terrain SR series); now rides brevets in Switzerland.
- PBP history: DNF in 2007 80hr group (lost bike in transit, emergency-bought an ill-fitting bike, cold weather, numb hands); finished 2015 and 2019, both in the 80hr group, times 53–55 hours.
- Fast 80hr plan: start ~4–5pm day one; reach Villaines-la-Juhel control (220K) by 10-11pm; first sunrise (day two) somewhere between Tinténiac and Loudéac (~350-400K covered); arrive Brest mid-afternoon day two; back through Loudéac shortly after dark for a nap; second sunrise near Tinténiac (~350K to finish); arrive Paris as night falls on day three.
- Start-wave dynamics: 2015 "A wave" (first starters) — mostly race bikes, riders with personal support crews, minimal gear, frequent littering, fast/dangerous start (several crashes in first km), reached Fougères (~307K) in under 10 hours.
- 2019 "E wave" (with friend Brian) — still fast but less so than A, more variable skill/motivation, oddly competitive pacelines, had to ride at the front to hold pace.
- Wave gamesmanship: some riders wait for friends to register so they start in a later wave (psychological advantage of chasing rather than being chased) — can backfire if it costs too much energy.
- Rule of thumb: return leg is roughly 6 hours slower than the outbound leg (e.g., 22hr out → ~28hr back = 50hr total; 24hr out → ~54hr total), assuming no major mechanicals.
- Pros of 80hr: covers 180-200K in daylight on day one; strong pace-line effect (first 300K in ~10hrs in the right group); less crowded controls outbound and from Tinténiac/Fougères back to Paris if maintaining pace.
- Cons: very fast/scary start (2015 A-wave called one of the highest-adrenaline cycling experiences of his life; that year started at Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines with road furniture/lane dividers causing severe crashes, including broken forks); return leg meets the huge bulge of outbound riders around Carhaix/Loudéac plus oncoming headlight glare.
- Recommendation: pick 80hr if targeting a specific time goal (e.g., "Charlie Miller" sub-something) or confident in one fast 600K; otherwise other start times may be safer.
- Q&A: on whether all 80hr waves are frenetic — every wave has some racing-minded riders; A wave uniformly hard-charging/borderline dangerous, E wave much more varied. On Charlie Miller attempts specifically, Max would avoid the later waves (D, E) and pick A or B — the first 20-30K are the riskiest part of the decision.
- Personal note: badly broke his leg in a collision with a badger during a 400K in fall 2020, still recovering mentally; hopeful for PBP 2023.

**Deb Banks — 90-Hour Start**
- Second-time presenter; RUSA board member for six years (just completed sixth year), current chair of RUSA's gravel committee (gravel program news coming later in the year).
- Riding brevets since 2007; holds the Mondial Award and R-60 status, completed a Super Series (600K+), earned the Ultra Randonneur Award; two-time PBP finisher (2007 finished 89:50, ~10 min to spare; 2011 finished 89:45).
- Rode the same 2007 PBP as Max and finished despite the bad weather that caused his DNF — cites the 90hr window as "the best insurance policy" against mechanicals, digestive issues, or weather.
- 90hr benefits: full night's sleep before start, afternoon nap, a proper meal, time to see friends off in earlier waves, and to watch the 80hr wave depart (did this in 2011).
- Wave starts now depart roughly every 15 minutes, spreading the field faster than the old "stadium bulge" system (pre-wave era) where riders queued for hours to get chip/card signed.
- 2019 "J wave" — spread out within about 1.5 hours of the start.
- Controls more crowded than smaller starts, but organizers have improved food/line management, especially outbound; on return, riders of different start times converge at the same controls simultaneously.
- With ~7,500 total riders, PBP means riding alongside Danish, Taiwanese, Japanese, Spanish, and French randonneurs in the same event — a unique international mix.
- Bathrooms described as consistently messy regardless of start time — bring your own toilet paper/wipes.
- Anecdote: in 2011, a young French man rode alongside her group, and it turned out his grandfather (who'd ridden PBP in the 1970s) joined briefly on the roadside to cheer him on — she called it an incredible, memorable moment.
- The Society of Ancien Hands (honor for slow/full-value finishers): named for Alan Hands ("NCN"), rode PBP 2003, believed every ride should be an adventure enjoyed to the fullest — full value finishing near the 90-hour cutoff qualifies. Deb is a two-time "Ancien Hands" style finisher — 2007 at 89:50, 2011 at 89:45.
- Q&A: only got a question about a rear rack pictured in her slides (a Jandd Bean rack) — noted it was actually her partner's setup, not hers; advises everyone to find gear/setup they're comfortable with well before France.

**Mary Gersma — 84-Hour Start**
- Rides with DC Randonneurs, usually tandem with husband Ed Falker; rode PBP 2011 and 2019 together on tandem, both times via the 84hr start, finishing both times within about 20 minutes of each other (2011 was ~20 min faster than 2019); finish time was 81.5 hours.
- Randonneuring since 2005; describes transportation cycling as her "first love."
- Field-size context (last PBP cited): 917 riders in the 84hr group (smallest), 4,500+ in the 90hr group, ~1,200 in the 80hr — and DNF rate appeared to increase with each shorter/later start group, though she wasn't sure of the cause.
- 84hr mechanics: Sunday bike inspection (vs. Saturday for others); starts at 5am the next morning after the 80s/90s have gone; 39 hours allotted to reach Brest, with expanding control windows on the return leg to Rambouillet.
- Rode much of 2019 with Rob Hawks, Anson, Brian, and Roy — called it a great experience.
- Start atmosphere: much smaller/lower-key than 80/90hr fanfare — described as feeling "like a DC Randonneurs brevet" at first, though 2019 had a more festive start banner than 2011's low-key "just go, don't hit anything" send-off.
- Rode early miles with Doug McLaren (Chicago). Loved passing through Loudéac both ways — big crowds cheering, kids helping carry food trays at the control.
- Advantages: maximizes daylight riding; quieter controls, though controls can start shutting down even with time still on the clock, particularly in Brest; can watch both the 80hr and 90hr waves depart if staying near the start.
- Registration note: 84hr group was easier to get into late in registration last time — some riders ended up there because it was the only slot left, not by first choice.
- Because 84hr riders start a day behind, they spend the first ~2 days of riding catching up to and passing through the tail end of the 90hr field — a "surreal" feeling requiring extra care at night around very fatigued riders who've been on the road much longer.
- Highlight: reaching the summit of Roc Trévezel and seeing the 90hr riders coming back through at the same time — called one of the best moments of the ride.
- Time budget: 39 hours to Brest, then 45 hours back to Rambouillet.
- Runs a blog called "Chasing Mailboxes" with rider PBP trip reports sorted by start wave, welcomes submissions.
- Q&A: someone asked about time splits — her tandem averaged 15 mph over the first day, 13.5 mph for the rest; targeted 3-4 hours of sleep per night. Also noted 2019 nights were colder than expected — her husband Ed left a jacket behind and regretted it (jacket turned out to be safe at home in a closet the whole time).

**Mark Thomas — Course Profile / Climbing ("Ups and Downs of PBP")**
- Riding PBP since 1999, finished every edition since, typically in the 85-90 hour range, with one outlier in 2015 at 55 hours.
- Rob noted Mark may be the only person to hold both the Ancien Hands and Charlie Miller Society awards (a very small ~4-person club) — the most notable other member being Alan Hands's son, Ian, himself.
- PBP has no major climbs but is emphatically not flat — deep into day two or three it "starts to feel like a hill fest."
- Total elevation gain roughly 11,000-13,000 meters depending on measurement method; his own GPS-recorded 2019 ride logged 11,850 meters. Pre-GPS era he estimated 10,000m, but no GPS-recorded ride has come in that low.
- Comparative climbing rate: ~10 meters/km (about 50 ft/mile) on PBP, versus ~17 m/km or more on a Super Randonneur 600K, and under 5 m/km on the Florida Cracker Swamp 1200K. Comparable to (slightly more climbing than) the Colorado High Country 1200.
- Elevation range is narrow: lowest point is sea level in Brest (just after crossing the bridge along the river) — but the actual Brest control itself sits atop roughly a 100-meter climb from there, which he called painful after 600K. Highest point is Roc Trévezel at ~330 meters (1,100 ft) — steeper from the west but even that side is only about 11km at ~2% grade; described as a fun spot with campers, tables, and people cheering riders at the summit.
- Steepest climbs are around 10%, and none are very long — the real challenge is the relentlessness/frequency of climbs rather than steepness; almost no flat stretches anywhere on the course.
- Breaks the route into three segments: Start→Loudéac (440km, ~9 m/km climbing, below average), Loudéac→Brest→Loudéac (340km "out and back" through Brest, ~11 m/km, the hilliest section — Brest to Carhaix segment closer to 12 m/km), and Loudéac→Finish.
- On his fast (55-hour) PBP, his Start→Loudéac pace was nearly 29 km/h, dropping substantially afterward due to terrain/fatigue.
- Training advice: don't avoid hills in training; consider riding a 300K or 400K brevet with roughly 3,300m (11,000 ft) of climbing — comparable to a "hilly double century" — ideally one with many short punchy climbs (PBP-style) rather than a few long mountain passes, since the terrain "feels different" and better prepares you mentally; riders from flat regions have still finished PBP successfully.
- Emotional ups and downs: lows are often just fatigue/hunger in disguise — quotes a mentor's rule "never quit till you've had an apple turnover" (a pastry). Remedies: eat, nap (a nap may pay for itself in speed), find a friend to ride with, focus on the next control/town/hill instead of the whole remaining distance, let go of unrealistic pacing expectations or of a faster group.
- Highs: riding bikes in France with friends, meeting international randonneurs, enjoying the hospitality of French villagers who cheer riders day and night, and simply reveling in "that big randonneur party."

**Narayan Krishnamurthy — Interview Segment (three-time PBP finisher, 2011/2015/2019)**
- Started randonneuring in 2004 with BC Randonneurs, now rides with Seattle International Randonneurs; self-described "card-carrying member of the Cyclo Escargot" (slow riders' club), values camaraderie, convenience-store food, and event trinkets.
- First learned about PBP from Ken Peterson on a touring mailing list discussing brevets vs. permanents/audax rides, around 2003; did his first 200K in 2004 and noticed people still talking about PBP 2003 a year later — that intrigued him.
- Drawn to PBP specifically because, unlike normal road riding where cyclists get harassed, PBP is an event where the public celebrates cyclists.
- Favorite/funny memory: near a small town beyond Villaines-la-Juhel, stopped at a roadside stand about an hour ahead of cutoff, out of water; a crowd of ~10 locals gathered to ask questions (translated by a fellow French recumbent rider); after severe sleep deprivation, misspoke in French claiming his "husband is in London" instead of "wife," to the crowd's amused reaction — corrected himself to laughter.
- Rob shared a similar embarrassing-French anecdote from 2015 near Villaines-la-Juhel involving a public toilet setup and being recognized on a return pass through town.
- Reason for returning in 2015 (after 2011): first goal in 2011 was just to finish; he was too slow to savor towns/stops. Returned to ride smarter — better bike/equipment, and an explicit plan for which towns/experiences to prioritize (wanted more time in Villaines-la-Juhel, the Mortagne-au-Perche area, and Sizun).
- Villaines-la-Juhel described as an emotional highlight both ways — riders moved to tears by the crowd's applause on both outbound and return passes.
- Lesson learned the hard way: advice from a mentor was "don't mess around between Villaines-la-Juhel and Mortagne" — he did anyway on his first PBP and made the Mortagne control with only about three minutes to spare.
- Noted his longest ride ever before PBP 2011 was under 1,000km, so the final ~200km back was "truly uncharted territory."
- Each of his three PBPs felt completely distinct, not repetitive — cites varying weather each time: nasty rain part of 2011 that later cleared; gorgeous weather for most of 2015 with a brutal 8-hour bad stretch; unusually cold overnight lows in 2019.
- Biggest change in preparation between 2011 and 2015: realized off-the-bike conditioning (not just distance/saddle time) made the biggest difference for a slow rider — recommends core work (front and side planks, just a couple minutes a day) to reduce neck/back issues and finish in better shape.
- Confirmed he plans to return for PBP 2023 "even if I have to pedal one leg."

**Closing Rapid-Fire Q&A / Best-Question Round**
- **Max:** Best question was about wave choice for a Charlie Miller attempt — recommends A or B wave, avoid D/E; first 20-30km are the key risk factor.
- **Deb:** Only got one question, about a rear rack shown in her slides (a Jandd Bean rack) — clarified it was her partner's setup, not her own; stresses everyone should find and get comfortable with their own gear setup well before France.
- **Mary:** Fielded a question on time splits — 15 mph average day one, 13.5 mph average remaining days (tandem); slept 3-4 hours/night; biggest surprise was how cold 2019 nights were (led to the "Ed's jacket" story — left behind but ultimately never needed since it stayed safely in a closet at home).
- **Mark:** No direct terrain questions beyond his talk. Confirmed the 80hr/84hr start times are hard cutoffs, not soft goals — finishing at 81 hours on an 80hr start = DNF. On the 2023 start location: as of the seminar, the start had not yet been officially confirmed as Rambouillet, though it was expected/rumored, with an announcement forthcoming.
- **Narayan:** Fielded a general question to all panelists on fenders — recommends not mounting fenders unless rain is forecast (but bringing them to France just in case), given the amount of manure on French roads that combines badly with rain.
- **Rob (closing note):** Has personally experienced rain at every PBP he's attended (2007, 2011, 2015, 2019). Noted rain effects varied by start wave — in 2011 the 84hr group got notably more rain than the 80hr/90hr groups who'd started the day before.
- Max, on being asked if PBP is a "race": says it's not a race in the sense of everyone competing, but can be approached as an individual time-trial-style athletic challenge, or as a food/culture/social experience — and can be multiple things to the same person simultaneously. Charlie Miller attempts limit village interaction but still lead to lasting international friendships on the road.
