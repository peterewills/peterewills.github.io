**Video:** Randonneurs USA PBP Prep Interview Series #4, "Boyz On The Hoods" — host Rob Hawks interviews four Bay Area randonneurs who rode Paris-Brest-Paris (PBP) 2019 together as a group: Ian (PBP veteran, rode 2015 and 2019), Ben Goldenberg, Carlin, and Irving — collectively their fleche/brevet team name is "Boys on the Hoods." All four finished PBP 2019 together.

**How Each Got Into Randonneuring**
- **Ian**: Father was into Rivendell bikes and Bicycle Quarterly (predecessor to the current one); at Christmas 2009 read an article about PBP, looked up that 1200 km ≈ 750 miles and thought "that's ridiculous." Had only done one century (the Tucson Century) at that point. Attracted by vintage French-style bikes (self-described Francophile). Signed up for the Lighthouse 200K in 2010 as his first brevet — rode it solo with friend Milo, later rode 200Ks with his dad (Santa Rosa, Napa) and a tandem ride together.
- **Ian's brevet progression**: 2010 — 200/300/400K (suffered on the 400K, decided a 600K was "too hard"); 2011 — came back with improved lights/bike (his 2010 lights were so weak/narrow-beam he couldn't see around turns) and completed his first 600K, riding with John Potus(?), Alex, Brian Clarkson, and a rider named Ricardo. Didn't feel ready for PBP in 2011; spent 2011–2015 with what he called a "burning passion/obsession" to do PBP, finally going in 2015.
- **Irving**: Met Ian around 2011–2012 at Bridge Plaza/Crissy Field (recognized him as "another youthful guy in jorts on a cool bike"). Didn't actually become friends until they were all "zonked" at a Pacifica 7-Eleven at 3am after a fleche, around age 20–22, sleep-deprived and talking; started riding fleches together after that.
- **"Boys on the Hoods" origin**: Irving got into randonneuring via Brian Wei, who'd moved to the Bay Area and convinced him to join; their first ride together was a Davis night 200K ("absolutely bonkers" but they survived). The team name was coined while climbing Mount Diablo on a hot training ride, brainstorming fleche team names — a play on the film title "Boyz n the Hood," reflecting that they weren't racing or riding hard ("not in the drops"), just riding long and having fun.
- **Carlin**: Road cycling background, riding with coworkers doing "fast roadie rides." Met Brian Wei and Irving through mutual friends; Brian was enthusiastic about randonneuring as a way to see the Bay Area outside a car. First met Ian on a "Mission Cycling Club" fast Tuesday-morning ride up Hawk Hill — Ian stood out riding a steel bike with a box bag among the Lycra crowd, which started their friendship.
- **Ben Goldenberg**: Came to randonneuring later than the others. First populaire ridden Summer 2013 (organized by Francisco Grajales); before that his longest ride was a "Paradise Loop." First 200K brevet fall 2014. Decided he needed a steel randonneuring bike. Got hooked as a structured way to push himself athletically and explore the Bay Area without it feeling intimidating; started riding fleches the following year or two.

**Deciding to Ride PBP 2019 as a Group**
- Carlin credits Ian's 2015 PBP experience/planning as the foundation the group leaned on.
- Group philosophy: accepted that at any given time some riders would feel great and others terrible, so no rigid pact to stay together — instead, overnight controls were the designated "anchor points" to regroup, reassess, and try to leave together.
- Ian had tried to ride PBP together with a rider named Gabe in 2015 and found it hard to stay together the whole way (they split for stretches). That taught the group of four for 2019: know you can separate on the road but plan to regroup at controls; lodging arranged in advance — two nights in Loudéac and a third night in an Airbnb — since being at fixed points made it possible to catch up with each other.
- Reasons the group actually split on the road: not usually climbing pace, but mental/physical state. Carlin developed knee pain trying to hold group pace and found pedaling harder with less spinning caused less pain, so he pulled ahead and let the others catch up at controls. Irving got sleepy and wanted to "ditch nap" at a random spot; Ian talked him into riding further to find a safer napping spot.

**Training and Preparation**
- The four did many long training rides together beforehand, especially fleches (24-hour team events) and 600Ks, which built social familiarity — knowing "how does Ian behave when things get tough," recognizing Irving's or Ben's moods, who likes playing music on Bluetooth speakers, etc.
- Carlin dealt with recurring knee pain after 600Ks in the years leading up to PBP; in the year before PBP he cut back on riding volume and focused on strength conditioning and physical therapy instead. Ironically, his 600K in 2019 was pain-free — the knee pain returned as a surprise during PBP itself.
- Carlin's PBP start was complicated: knee issues limited him to only a 200K in 2018, so he wasn't seeded in the same start wave as the others and began roughly three hours after them, riding solo pace for the first 200–300K before meeting up with the group. Knee pain became bad in the middle section; out of frustration he chased down a fast group of 80-hour starters passing by, stood on the pedals, and found it actually relieved the pain.
- Irving/Carlin: watching Ian's excitement after his 2015 PBP (including seeing him off/greeting him at the airport) created some FOMO and motivation, plus a strong desire that pursuing PBP together wouldn't fracture their friendships. Self-doubt centered on whether they could handle a 600K and function on minimal sleep; working through that fear built PBP readiness.
- **Scheduling as key prep**: Ian described doing a "workers ride" for a 400K with Kevin Fitzpatrick after his 2015 PBP (where he'd had no schedule and no idea how long he could stop/sleep). Fitzpatrick rode a deliberately slow but tightly scheduled pace, arriving at controls within five minutes of his planned times. This inspired Ian to build a detailed schedule for PBP 2019, letting the group know exactly what pace/stops were needed to finish and adjust dynamically if something went wrong, while still meeting at controls together. The group had practiced this schedule discipline on fleches beforehand.
- A shared Excel spreadsheet schedule tool (mentioned by host Rob) was used by many riders that year to plan PBP pacing.

**Schedule Discipline and Falling Behind**
- Irving lost time on the 600K due to a saddle issue, arriving roughly two hours after the others at the Indian Creek campground — framed as good practice for accepting less rest before facing real PBP sleep deprivation.
- Consensus: the critical rule of thumb is knowing when you must leave a control — losing time (even from going off-course) is tolerable as long as you know your control-departure deadlines and can still finish on schedule.
- Ian: in 2015 he had no schedule and felt "on the edge," emotionally intense and serious about whether he'd finish. In 2019, having a schedule and hitting his control marks let him relax more and actually enjoy watching the spectacle of PBP — people from all over the world in various states of distress, speaking different languages, which he found genuinely funny.
- All four chose the 90-hour start (none did 84-hour). One rider (wanting "the full spectacle") was encouraged by a teammate to consider the 84-hour start but stuck with 90 hours, and was glad — night riding turned out to be a personal highlight, especially the first night rolling out at dusk into pace lines, riding through towns with big cheering crowds at sunset.

**Ben's Ongoing Randonneuring**
- Ben now lives in Sacramento; has ridden a gravel populaire a couple months prior to the interview and one or two 200Ks in the past year — describing brevets as a good way to explore local areas with the right amount of pre-set structure.

**Memorable Moments at PBP 2019**
- **Irving**: Rolling in together to the finish; seeing host Rob Hawks at the Dreux control (likening it to "the Super Bowl of randonneuring") and appreciating the volunteer effort; riding into the finish together as a group despite Carlin's knee pain and his own hand/feet pain and self-doubt along the way.
- **Ian**: Staying in an Airbnb in Mortagne (Mortagne-au-Perche) — an old French house with an antique key he and Carlin couldn't work; around midnight/1am, drunk French teenagers passed by and used broken French communication to help fiddle the door open. No sheets or towels in the rental (guests were supposed to bring their own), but it was indoors.
- **Ben**: The first night riding into the sunset; also napping at a control — sleeping on cardboard under a stairwell, and separately sleeping bolt upright on a bench alongside eight other sleep-deprived riders.
- **Carlin**: Food from unofficial roadside spectators/tents (official control food was underwhelming) — a standout memory of eating delicious watermelon on a hillside overlooking a sunflower field at sunset, calling it "the best watermelon I've ever eaten." Broader takeaway: easy to get lost in logistics/fitness anxiety and forget PBP is fundamentally a multi-day bike ride through beautiful scenery with friends — even riders who DNF (e.g., after 900K) still have an amazing experience.
- Rob shared a photo of Brian Kilgore (a friend, referenced as "RIP lucky dog") roughly two days into the ride at that point in the photo.

**Parting Comments**
- Irving: Loved simply being on a bike in the French countryside in summer, and was struck by the people met along the way — bonding instantly with other English-speaking riders/clubs, including meeting the "Asheville crew" and other people previously known only online.
- One rider (self-doubt/anxiety theme, likely Carlin): Dealt with significant self-doubt and anxiety leading into the ride, got caught up in logistics-driven anxiety, but found that pushing through the ups and downs of PBP was itself the confidence-building experience — surprising yourself with what you can do after already doing a brevet or populaire.
- On randonneuring broadly: it offers unusual amounts of time with people, deepening friendships; conversation while pedaling is a great social outlet, and training for PBP together became a bonding mechanism for the group of four beyond just the riding itself (also hanging out, talking logistics, grabbing drinks).
- Also noted: fun hanging out with the SFR (San Francisco Randonneurs) crew and feeling their collective nervous energy before the start; emphasized there are many valid ways to train for PBP — from riding constantly beforehand to structured plans to (for e.g. Minnesota riders) not getting into long rides until snow thaws, and it can all still work out.

**Rapid-Fire / Closing Q&A**
- **Baguette-mouth injuries** (prompted by Rob's story of a post-PBP-2007 conversation about underreported PBP injuries like hand numbness/neuropathy and Shermer's neck): one rider said the roof of his mouth was torn up for weeks from eating so many baguettes.
- Anecdote: at the finish, this rider's girlfriend (now wife), Sarah, brought him a grocery bag of food including pineapple — his favorite fruit — but biting into it caused his raw baguette-mouth to "explode in pain."
- Group noted some randonneurs cope by dipping their baguette in Coca-Cola to soften it.
- After PBP, Irving, Ben, and a third rider stayed in France, traveling to the Pyrenees (Luchon area) to ride the Tourmalet climb — food there was "not amazing," mostly pizza and baguettes.
- Future PBP plans: Ian said yes immediately — his stated life goal is to finish PBP five times, though he's more accepting of a potential DNF than he used to be (referencing a past ride, possibly near Boonville, where he had to DNF but still had a fun, shorter-than-expected ride). Another rider said yes to riding PBP again, though not in 2023 and possibly not even 2027.
- Rob closed by noting the guests are roughly half his age (he didn't start randonneuring until age 50), thanking them for their time away from family/evening schedules.
